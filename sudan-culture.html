The land behind every harvest

Sudan, in six places and a thousand small hands

Before it reaches your kitchen, every gum arabic tear, every dried hibiscus flower, every handwoven basket has already travelled through a Sudan most of Europe never sees. Here's a closer look at the regions our cooperatives call home — their land, their light, and the work that happens there long before anything ships.

A short journey through Sudan — the land, the markets and the hands behind every harvest.

Where the gum arabic trees grow

Kordofan

Kordofan is the heartland of Sudan's gum arabic belt, a wide stretch of acacia woodland where families have tapped the same trees for generations. Villages here rise with the sun and work by it too — sorting resin by hand, grading it by color and clarity, and drying it in open courtyards before it ever sees a scale. It's slow, patient work, done mostly by women who know each tree the way others know a street they grew up on. Fatima's cooperative, the one behind your gum arabic, is rooted here.

Sahel, savanna and centuries of craft

Darfur

Darfur stretches from dry savanna to volcanic highlands, and its markets are some of the liveliest in the country — stalls of woven mats, dyed textiles and leatherwork passed down through families of artisans. Despite hardship the region has known, its craft traditions have held: basket weaving, pottery and embroidery still done exactly as they were a century ago. It's a landscape of resilience, and every handwoven piece that leaves it carries that history stitched into it.

Where the Taka hills meet the market square

Kassala

Kassala sits beneath the jagged Taka hills near the Eritrean border, its skyline unlike anywhere else in Sudan. The city is known for its fruit and spice markets, fragrant with cardamom, cinnamon and dried citrus, and for the henna and textile trade that draws traders from across the region. Life moves to the rhythm of the souq here — early mornings, unhurried bargaining, tea shared between strangers. Some of our spice blends begin in exactly this kind of market stall.

The green corridor along the river

Al-Dender

Al-Dender lies along a tributary of the Blue Nile, where the land turns unexpectedly green — reed beds, grazing cattle, and farmland that floods and blooms with the seasons. It's one of the quieter regions we work with, known for its wildlife-rich riverbanks and small farming communities that grow peanuts and sesame using methods barely changed in decades. The pace here is set by the river, not the clock.

The gateway to the Red Sea

Port Sudan

Port Sudan is where the country meets the sea — a working port city of cranes, fishing boats and coral reefs just offshore that rank among the least disturbed in the world. It's also the last stop for nearly everything we ship: every batch of gum arabic, hibiscus and spice we send to Europe passes through these docks before it reaches open water. Behind the industry, the old town still keeps a slower, coastal rhythm.

Pyramids older than most of Europe's cities

Meroe

Long before it was part of any modern trade route, this stretch of desert along the Nile was the seat of an ancient kingdom — its pyramids, sharper and more numerous than Egypt's, still rise quietly from the sand. Meroe is a reminder of how old Sudan's story really is, and how much has passed through this land before a single one of our products ever did. We think of it as the quiet backdrop to everything else in this list.

Faith, woven into daily life

Religions of Sudan

Islam is the majority faith across Sudan, shaping the rhythm of daily life from the call to prayer that marks each day to the festivals that bring whole communities together, like Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. Sufi traditions run especially deep here, with local orders known for their music, poetry and communal gatherings that feel as much like celebration as worship. Christianity has its own long history in the country too, practiced mainly among communities in the south and among diaspora congregations in cities like Khartoum, with churches that have stood for generations. Across both traditions, faith in Sudan tends to be lived communally — shared meals, shared prayer, and a hospitality that welcomes neighbors regardless of which mosque or church they belong to.

Music, food and the everyday art of hospitality

Culture

Sudanese culture is built on hospitality — a stranger is rarely allowed to leave a home without being offered tea, and often a full meal besides. Music runs through everyday life too, from the string-driven sounds of oud and tambour to wedding songs that can last long into the night. Food carries its own traditions: kisra and ful medames on family tables, spiced coffee poured with ceremony, and dishes that change slightly from Kordofan to the Nile valley to the Red Sea coast. Traditional dress remains part of daily life as well, from the flowing white jalabiya worn by men to the colorful toub wrapped by women, patterns and colors that often carry regional meaning. It's a culture shaped as much by generosity and gathering as by any single tradition.

The many communities behind one country

People

Sudan is home to an extraordinary mix of communities — Arab, Nubian, Beja, Fur, Nuba and dozens of other ethnic groups, each with its own language, customs and history stretching back centuries. That diversity shows up in everything from the dialects spoken in a single market to the patterns woven into a basket or a scarf. What ties these communities together is often less about shared background and more about shared values: a deep respect for elders, warmth toward guests, and a sense of community that shapes how people work, celebrate and get through hard seasons together. The cooperatives behind Nileva's products reflect that same diversity — women from different regions and backgrounds, working side by side.
